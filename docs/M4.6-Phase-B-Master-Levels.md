## Dependency design and meaningful movement

These chains describe the stored witnesses. Alternate legal routes and orders are
allowed; a blocked direct route does not prove every solution uses that route.
Neither board permits any initial straight one-action exit, checked with the actual model.

**Chain Reaction** has two coupled transfer systems. In the east, F initially prevents
R reaching its lower parking place. B parks at `(1,0)`, F descends and moves to `(2,0)`,
then R can descend, opening D's route to the Purple gate. R parks at `(6,0)` to make room
for E to descend. F next lifts to `(2,1)`, opening the bottom route for B; B must move
before C can leave. Only after C exits can F shift left to `(0,1)`.

The west then needs a separate staged sequence. P rises from `(1,4)` back to `(1,5)`
so G can lift to `(0,4)` and F can advance to `(0,2)`. E can now cross west at row 1,
opening the right-hand northern route for B. E exits and R transfers into the lower
left parking place. A moves away from its own South alignment, P temporarily returns
to `(2,5)`, and G/F/R can use the Green passage in order. Only then can A return and
P take its lower exit route. Moving E left too soon is blocked by F; sending F left
before C leaves is blocked by C. These restrictions are regression-tested.

**Final Lockdown** adds a shared narrow-lane bottleneck. Blue H occupies the bottom
transfer, B starts in C's desired staging location, and Amber I occupies the upper
right bay. F descends, H shifts left, B moves down and C shifts west before K can leave.
As in Chain, F/R/D must then stage the eastern route. I descends and parks at `(6,2)`:
that is a temporary side pocket, not an exit route. It cannot move down while R is below.

E can now descend, but its lower path is still blocked by the left traffic. P rises,
G lifts, and C must move **up** to `(0,3)`, away from its South gate, to let F and H
reposition. Only then can E cross and exit, followed by B using the newly cleared
northern route. F returns to the bottom and parks at `(3,0)`, H briefly shifts right,
C finally exits, and G parks down at `(0,0)`. H shifts back left; P must temporarily
park at `(0,5)` to give the tall Blue block the entire left vertical passage. H can
then rise, align and escape. It cannot use the single-row passage above the spine
because its V2 footprint spans two rows.

That opens another handoff: P returns, G lifts, F crosses left, I returns from its side
pocket and descends, F rises out of its way, and I crosses the bottom and exits South.
R finally crosses west. A and P then stage the Green route so G/F can exit, P takes
its own Purple route, R exits, and A returns to its South alignment. Several locally
attractive moves, such as sending I down before relocating R or trying to empty C's
lane before H is staged, are blocked. Emptying a space is insufficient unless the next
piece still has somewhere to park. This supplies planning beyond the extra pieces.

The timers provide thinking time. They do not substitute for these constraints.
Movement uses full grid cells and the accepted per-cell pointer/collider interaction;
there is no pixel-perfect target, hidden rotation or new dragging capability.

## Solver, shortening and actual difficulty bounds

The accepted action definition remains unchanged: one positive straight axis request
to any reachable resting anchor, or one straight request that exits a block. A long
slide counts once. A blocked zero-step request does not count. A held physical drag
can change axes over multiple frames and therefore contain several actions. Grid-step
totals below count full anchor travel, including the fully outside end of each exit.

The existing deterministic solver generates every reachable anchor along each cardinal
direction using **`GridBoard.MoveWithExits`**, including occupancy, real L offsets,
obstacles, matching gate colors, aperture fit and full exit sweep. It has no second
collision model. Heuristics sum exact independent-piece distances through the same
obstacles and gates, ignoring other pieces. This is an admissible, consistent lower
bound. Weight 1 is A* and may prove optimality; larger weights return witnesses only.
State keys, block/direction enumeration and heap tie-breaks are deterministic.

The new `SimplifyWitness` authoring pass tries deleting each single non-exit action
and every pair of non-exit actions, replaying the entire candidate through `PuzzleSession`
and checking exact resting anchors, exit statuses and final Won state. It repeats until
no such deletion succeeds. This removes gratuitous return-and-repark loops; it does not
prove global optimality or rule out a differently planned route. The 41-action witness
was unchanged; Final's raw 66-action result became 64 after removing two redundant A moves.
No extra actions were inserted to hit a requested target.

| Level | Stored valid witness | Grid steps | Verified lower bound | Shortest solution status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chain Reaction | **41 actions** | 119 | **32** | minimum in **[32,41]**, not proved |
| Final Lockdown | **64 actions** | 168 | **40** | minimum in **[40,64]**, not proved |

The accepted Grand Escape witness remains 32 actions/91 grid steps, unchanged in its
source file. The new shortening pass can reduce a search-produced Grand witness to
30; this is analysis only, not a layout or gameplay change. Chain's lower bound is
therefore above a known Grand solution, and Final requires at least ten more actions
than that known solution. Human difficulty is not proved by move counts. The staged
parking chains, closed tall-piece bypass and much larger search effort provide
additional evidence; manual puzzle evaluation remains necessary.

## Responsive presentation and chosen final dimensions

The accepted camera/board presentation code is unchanged. Both new boards use its
safe-area gameplay rectangle (316 reference pixels above, 72 below, 32 at each side),
continuous whole-block/burst envelope and actual camera orientation. Their 5.44×7.48
world-unit grids fit the unchanged 6×8-world-unit rounded tray. Existing pointer rays
still intersect the same XZ plane and convert through the actual root and cell size.

Both master boards measure identically in the tested framing cases, with 96px top
and 64px bottom safe-area exclusions:

| 1080×1920 | 7.8035 | 83.65 px | 74.82 px |
| 1080×2340 | 9.5732 | 83.11 px | 74.33 px |
| 1080×2400 | 9.8275 | 83.03 px | 74.27 px |

For the proposed 9×12 Final variant with the same footprints/gates and fitting envelope,
the 1080×1920 pitch becomes **77.28×69.12 px**, and the 6.12×8.16-world-unit grid exceeds
the accepted tray footprint. This is not a claim that every 9×12 game is unreadable;
8×11 was selected conservatively to retain the already approved Grand selection scale,
avoid altering the tray and preserve silhouette readability with twelve pieces. The
harder puzzle comes from dependencies rather than screen size.

Pixel pitches are not physical device-independent touch-target guarantees. Actual
single-cell selection, densely adjacent shapes and the L empty corner still need
mouse and touchscreen QA. There are no enlarged invisible targets, camera-angle
changes, material edits or hidden zoom mechanics. Continuous burst bounds are tested
at every matching gate alignment, including tall Blue and South-exiting Amber L.
